Are Rappers More Connected Than Random?

We scraped rapper Wikipedia pages, built a link network, and compared it to degree-preserving random shuffles. The real network has far more clustering and triangles than chance alone would produce. Click "Shuffle" to see it yourself.

TL;DR — 3 things we learned
  • Clustering is not random. The real rapper network has dramatically higher clustering than degree-preserving shuffles. Structure matters — who links to whom is not just a function of how many links each page has.
  • Triangles everywhere. Real networks close triangles far more often than random rewirings. If A links to B and B links to C, then A very likely links to C — a pattern destroyed by shuffling.
  • The friendship paradox is real. Most rappers' neighbors have higher average degree than they do. Your friends really are more popular than you.

Building the network

We used the Wikipedia API to collect pages from the "21st-century American rappers" category, then fetched every internal link from each page. An edge A→B is added only if both A and B are in our rapper set. After removing isolates (pages with no connections to other rappers), we collapse the directed graph to undirected for metric comparison.

What makes a network "real"?

A network's structure could be an accident of degree: maybe high-degree nodes just happen to form triangles because they have so many links. To test this, we use a null model: a degree-preserving random shuffle. We repeatedly swap edges (A→B, C→D becomes A→D, C→B) while keeping every node's degree exactly the same. This destroys community structure and clustering while preserving the degree sequence.

If the real network's clustering is much higher than the shuffled versions, we know the structure is not just a side-effect of the degree distribution. Something is actually causing rappers to cluster.

Statistical significance

A z-score measures how many standard deviations the real value lies from the null model mean. Anything above 2 is conventionally "significant" — the real value is unlikely to arise by chance. An empirical p-value of 0 means not a single null model run matched or exceeded the real value.

Clustering (z ≈ —) and triangles (z ≈ —) are massively significant: the real network has far more local structure than any degree-preserving random graph could produce. Assortativity, path length, and modularity are also significant, confirming that the wiring pattern is not a side-effect of the degree sequence.

Is it scale-free?

A complementary cumulative distribution function (CCDF) plots P(K ≥ k) on log-log axes. If the degree distribution follows a power law, the CCDF should appear as a straight line. We compare the rapper network's in-degree CCDF (red) against a Barabási-Albert (BA) model with the same N and average degree (blue).

The CCDF avoids the binning artefacts of histograms and makes heavy tails more visible. On a log-log plot, a power-law tail appears as a straight line with slope −(α−1). With only 254 nodes, finite-size effects blur the tail, so we shouldn't over-interpret the exact exponent.

Small-world signature

A small-world network has clustering much higher than a random graph of the same size, while maintaining a comparably short average path length. The classic way to test this is with a Watts-Strogatz sweep: we generate ring-lattice graphs with the same N and average degree, then increase the rewiring probability q from 0 (regular lattice) to 1 (random graph).

The plot below shows normalized clustering C(q)/C(0) and normalized path length L(q)/L(0) as q increases. The "sweet spot" of small-world networks is where L has already dropped but C remains high. The rapper network's position is marked with a diamond.

Communities

The greedy modularity algorithm finds communities in the undirected network. These clusters often map to real-world connections: geography (East Coast vs West Coast), era (90s vs 2010s), or record label affiliations. The community colors in the interactive above reflect these detected groups.

The friendship paradox

The friendship paradox states that, on average, your friends have more friends than you do. In our rapper network, we measured the fraction of rappers whose neighbors have a higher average degree than they do. The result: — of rappers experience the friendship paradox. This is a well-known property of networks with heavy-tailed degree distributions — high-degree nodes appear in many neighborhoods, pulling up the average.

What surprised us

Three things we did not expect

1. Clustering survives shuffling poorly. The null model produces near-zero clustering even though every node keeps its exact degree. Structure is not a side-effect of popularity.

2. The friendship paradox is strong. In most real networks, 60-80% of nodes experience it. Our rapper network is no exception — the paradox is a mathematical consequence of degree variance, not a cultural observation.

3. Assortativity flips sign. The real network can be disassortative (popular rappers link to less popular ones), while shuffled versions tend toward zero. The mixing pattern is a real structural feature.

Method & data

  • Data: Rapper pages scraped from Wikipedia's "21st-century American rappers" category via the MediaWiki API. Directed edges = internal wiki-links between pages in our set.
  • Null model: Degree-preserving double edge swap (nx.double_edge_swap), 10× edges per shuffle. 20 shuffles pre-computed; live shuffle runs in-browser via JS.
  • Tools: Python / NetworkX for graph analysis and community detection; D3.js v7 for every visualisation on this page.
  • Community detection: Greedy modularity maximisation (nx.community.greedy_modularity_communities) on the undirected projection.
  • Reading: Barabási, Network Science, ch. 3 (Random networks) and ch. 4 (Scale-free property). Watts & Strogatz (1998) on small-world networks.
